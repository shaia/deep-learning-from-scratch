## Finding structure in time

In 1990 the journal *Cognitive Science* published a paper by Jeffrey Elman, a linguist at the
University of California, San Diego, with a deceptively modest title: *Finding Structure in Time*. Its question was how a neural network should represent time at all. The
usual answer in the 1980s was to turn time into space. NETtalk, the famous network of 1987 that
learned to read English aloud, saw text through a window seven letters wide: the whole window was
one input vector, and the network had no idea that the letters in it had come in an order. Elman
pointed out what that costs. The window has a fixed length, so nothing outside it can matter; and
the same word, shifted by one position, is an entirely different input pattern that must be
learned again from scratch.

His proposal was to let time be represented by its *effect* on processing. Give the network a
memory: a set of "context units" that hold a copy of the hidden layer from the previous step and
feed it back in, alongside the next input. The network reads a sequence one item at a time, and
what it has read so far lives in its hidden state. Elman trained such a network to do one thing —
predict the next item — and then looked inside.

The results are still striking. Fed a stream of letters from simple English sentences with the
spaces removed, the network's prediction error rose at the start of each word and fell steadily
through it: it had found the word boundaries, which nobody had marked, from the statistics of the
stream alone. Fed sentences one word at a time, its hidden states clustered by grammatical role —
nouns apart from verbs, animate nouns apart from inanimate ones, humans apart from animals —
categories nobody had given it, discovered because they help predict what comes next.

This chapter builds Elman's network and the training method that makes it work in full,
backpropagation through time, which Paul Werbos published the same year. It shows what a
recurrent network can learn and what it cannot, measures *why* — a gradient that shrinks or grows
geometrically as it travels back in time — and ends with a model that writes Shakespeare one
character at a time, and rediscovers Elman's word boundaries on the way.

## Memory, 1982–2015

Recurrent networks are as old as neural networks; the trouble was always training them.

In 1982 John Hopfield described a network in which every unit feeds every other, and showed that
it settles into stored patterns like a ball rolling into a valley — a memory, but of states, not
sequences. In the 1986 volumes of *Parallel Distributed Processing* that made backpropagation
famous (Module 01), Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams already noted that a recurrent network can be
*unfolded* in time into a layered network whose layers share their weights, and trained like one.
Michael Jordan, the same year, fed a network's output back into it as a state. In 1989 Alex Waibel
and colleagues took the other road for speech: the *time-delay neural network* slid the same
weights along the time axis — a convolution in time, Module 03's weight sharing on a fixed
window. Ronald Williams and David Zipser found a way to train fully recurrent networks *online*,
carrying gradients forward as the sequence arrives.

Then 1990: Elman's simple recurrent network, and Werbos's tutorial, *Backpropagation Through Time:
What It Does and How to Do It*. Werbos — who had proposed backpropagation itself in his 1974
thesis — had already used it on a recurrent model of the natural-gas market in 1988. Elman trained
his network with only the last step of it: the context units were treated as just another input,
and the gradient stopped there.

The next discovery was bad news. In his 1991 diploma thesis in Munich, Sepp Hochreiter analysed
how the error signal behaves as it is propagated back through many time steps, and found that it
either vanishes exponentially or blows up. Three years later Yoshua Bengio, Patrice Simard and
Paolo Frasconi proved that this is not an accident of a bad initialization: a recurrent network
that stores information *robustly* — so that small noise cannot erase it — is exactly one whose
gradients from the distant past vanish. Learning long-range dependencies by gradient descent was,
in their title's word, *difficult*. The fix (Module 07) came in 1997, and recurrent networks spent
the next decade as a specialist's tool.

They came back with language. In 2010 Tomáš Mikolov's recurrent language model beat the n-gram
counts that had dominated speech recognition for decades. In 2011 Ilya Sutskever — whose reading
list this book follows — with James Martens and Geoffrey Hinton trained a recurrent network on
Wikipedia one character at a time and had it write. In 2013 Razvan Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio
explained the exploding half of Hochreiter's problem as cliffs in the loss surface and made
gradient clipping standard. And in May 2015 Andrej Karpathy published *The Unreasonable
Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks*, with code and samples — fake Shakespeare, fake
Wikipedia, fake Linux source — that made character-level models the "hello world" of deep learning
for text. His Tiny Shakespeare is this chapter's dataset.

## The intuition: one rule, applied at every step

Think of reading a novel. You do not reread from page one before every new page; you carry a
running summary — who is where, who wants what — and update it as each sentence comes in, by the
same habits of reading on page 3 as on page 300. A recurrent network is that. Its hidden state is
the running summary; at every step it combines the summary with the next input to make the next
summary, using **one set of weights** for every step. Nothing about the rule depends on the
position, so the network handles a sentence of four words or a book of four million characters
with the same parameters, and what it learns at one position it knows at every other — Module 03's
weight sharing, across time instead of across an image.

Unroll the reading and it is a deep network: one layer per step, every layer identical. Training
it is backpropagation through that unrolled network, and it carries an obvious difficulty. Suppose
the summary at page 300 lacks something that was on page 3. The blame has to travel back 297 steps,
through 297 copies of the same layer, to reach the moment the summary should have kept it. Each
step multiplies the blame by the same matrix. Multiply anything by the same factor 297 times and it
is either nearly zero or astronomically large; only a factor of exactly one survives. That single
fact — **the gradient through time is a power of a matrix** — is the subject of most of this chapter,
and the reason the next one exists.

## The math: recurrence, and backpropagation through time

Notation as in the notation guide: row vectors times weight matrices, rows = samples, and the
superscript $\langle t\rangle$ for time. A sequence is $x^{\langle 0\rangle}, \ldots, x^{\langle T-1\rangle}$, each
$x^{\langle t\rangle}$ a batch of $m$ inputs; for characters, a one-hot vector over the vocabulary.

### The recurrence

The hidden state starts at $h^{\langle -1\rangle} = h_0$ (usually zeros) and is updated at every step:

$$
z^{\langle t\rangle} = x^{\langle t\rangle} W_{xh} + h^{\langle t-1\rangle} W_{hh} + b_h, \qquad
h^{\langle t\rangle} = \tanh\big(z^{\langle t\rangle}\big).
$$

A read-out turns each state into a prediction, $\hat y^{\langle t\rangle} = \mathrm{softmax}(h^{\langle t\rangle} W_{hy} + b_y)$,
and the loss averages the cross-entropy over every step that has a target. $W_{xh}$, $W_{hh}$ and $b_h$
are the same at every step. (Multiplying a one-hot $x$ by $W_{xh}$ simply picks one row of $W_{xh}$
— which is why frameworks implement it as a table lookup, an *embedding*.)

### Backpropagation through time

Unrolled, the recurrence is a $T$-layer network, and its backward pass needs only two rules this
book already has.

**A state is used twice** — by the read-out at its own step and by the next step. As at Module 05's
residual fork, its gradient is the sum of both routes. Walking backwards from the last step,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{\langle t\rangle}} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{\langle t\rangle}}\Big|_{\text{read-out}}}_{dH^{\langle t\rangle}}
\;+\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{\langle t+1\rangle}}\, W_{hh}^{\top},
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{\langle t\rangle}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{\langle t\rangle}} \odot \big(1 - (h^{\langle t\rangle})^2\big).
$$

**A weight is used at every step** — so, as for Module 03's convolution kernels, its gradient is the
sum of its gradients at every use:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{xh}} = \sum_t x^{\langle t\rangle\top}\frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{\langle t\rangle}}, \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{hh}} = \sum_t h^{\langle t-1\rangle\top}\frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{\langle t\rangle}}, \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_h} = \sum_t \sum_{\text{rows}} \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{\langle t\rangle}}.
$$

That is all of backpropagation through time: a loop over $t$ in reverse that carries one extra
gradient, from the future, and lets every step add its share to the shared weights.

### Truncation

Long texts are trained in chunks: read 64 characters, update, carry the hidden state into the next
chunk. The state — the memory — flows on across the cut; the gradient does not. That is
**truncated** backpropagation through time, and its truncation length $k$ is how far back any
training signal can reach. Elman's 1990 training is the extreme case $k = 1$: the previous state
is treated as just another input, and the gradient stops at it.

### The product, and the spectral radius

Follow a gradient back $k$ steps:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{\langle t-k\rangle}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{\langle t\rangle}}
\prod_{j=0}^{k-1} \mathrm{diag}\big(1 - (h^{\langle t-j\rangle})^2\big)\, W_{hh}^{\top}.
$$

A feed-forward network of depth $k$ multiplies $k$ *different* Jacobians. A recurrent network
multiplies the *same* $W_{hh}^{\top}$ again and again (the tanh slopes, all at most 1, can only shrink
it). So the gradient behaves like a power of a matrix: roughly like $\rho^k$, where $\rho$ is
$W_{hh}$'s **spectral radius**, the largest magnitude among its eigenvalues. Below 1 it vanishes
geometrically; above 1 it can explode. Xavier initialization — standard deviation $1/\sqrt{n_h}$ —
puts $\rho$ almost exactly at 1, because of a beautiful piece of random-matrix theory, the *circular
law*: the eigenvalues of an $n \times n$ matrix of independent entries with variance $1/n$ fill the
unit disc. But no fixed $\rho$ keeps $\rho^k$ near 1 for every $k$, and Bengio, Simard and Frasconi's
theorem says the dynamics a network needs to *store* information robustly are the ones that make it
vanish.

### Clipping

The exploding half has a cheap guard. Treat all the gradients as one long vector $g$; if its length
exceeds a threshold $\tau$, shrink it to $\tau$:

$$
g \leftarrow g \cdot \frac{\tau}{\|g\|} \qquad \text{if } \|g\| > \tau .
$$

Same direction, shorter step. Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio's argument for it is geometric, and the
notebook reproduces it: a recurrent network's loss surface has *cliffs*, where a small change in the
weights flips the long-run behaviour of the dynamics; a gradient step that lands on one is huge and
throws the parameters far away. Clipping does nothing on ordinary steps and turns that one into an
ordinary step.

## The code: a loop that runs backwards

The C library gains one file
([`lib/c/nanograd/rnn.c`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/lib/c/nanograd/rnn.c))
with the forward pass and backpropagation through time, and `ng_clip_grad_norm`. The heart of the
backward pass is the line that decides whether the gradient from step $t+1$ reaches step $t$:

```c
for (int t = T - 1; t >= 0; t--) {
    /* Does step t+1's gradient reach back to step t? Not past the end,
     * and not across a truncation cut. */
    const int flows = (t + 1 < T) && !(truncate > 0 && (t + 1) % truncate == 0);
    for (int i = 0; i < m; i++)
        for (int j = 0; j < n_h; j++) {
            double s = dh_out[i * n_h + j];                       /* from the read-out */
            if (flows)
                for (int o = 0; o < n_h; o++)
                    s += dz_next[i * n_h + o] * Whh[j * n_h + o];  /* from the future  */
            dz[i * n_h + j] = s * (1.0 - h[i * n_h + j] * h[i * n_h + j]);
        }
    /* ...then each shared weight adds this step's share: dWxh += x^T dz, ... */
}
```

`truncate = 1` is Elman's training; `0` is full backpropagation through time. The C program
([`c/rnn.c`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/06-rnn/c/rnn.c))
trains the same network both ways from the same initial weights; that one argument is the only
difference between them.

The Python layer, `nanograd.RNN`, reads the same, with one optimization the math allows: the input's
contribution $x^{\langle t\rangle} W_{xh}$ has no dependence on the past, so all $T$ steps of it are one matrix
product, and only the recurrence itself must be a loop:

```python
def backward(self, dH, truncate=0):
    dh_next = np.zeros_like(self.H[0])            # nothing flows in from after T
    for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
        dh = dH[t] + dh_next                      # from the output at t, and from t+1
        dZ[t] = dh * (1.0 - H[t] * H[t])          # back through tanh
        if truncate and t % truncate == 0:
            dh_next = np.zeros_like(dh_next)      # the cut: no further into the past
        else:
            dh_next = dZ[t] @ Whh.T               # one step further back
    # the shared weights: every step's share, summed in one product over T*m rows
    self.dWhh = H_prev.reshape(-1, n_h).T @ dZ.reshape(-1, n_h)
```

That `for` loop is also the recurrent network's great practical weakness. A convolution or a dense
layer processes a whole batch in one large matrix product, which is what GPUs are built for; a
recurrent layer must take $T$ small steps in order, each waiting for the last. Module 13's
Transformer is, among other things, a way out of that loop.

One detail of the agreement test is new. As in every module since 03, the Python side has a mirror of
the C toy that performs every floating-point operation in the C's order, so the two can be compared
to the last digit. A recurrent network adds a transcendental function to the loop — tanh — and NumPy
computes its own. Measured on 200,000 random inputs, `np.tanh` differs from the C library's `tanh`
in the last bit for **22%** of them, while Python's `math.tanh`, which calls the C library, matches
all of them. A one-ulp difference fed through a few hundred training steps of a recurrent network is
enough to change the printed digits, so the mirror evaluates tanh (and exp and log) through `math`.

## The gradient check, and C↔Python agreement

[`tests/test_agreement.py`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/06-rnn/tests/test_agreement.py)
holds the usual three gates.

**Gradients.** The recurrent layer's $\partial L/\partial X$, $\partial L/\partial h_0$ and its three weight
gradients are finite-difference checked with an initial state (errors around $10^{-9}$), and so are
small echo and counting networks end to end, every parameter. Truncation is checked against what it
*means*: the truncated gradient must equal the sum of the full gradients of the chunks, each chunk
starting from the state the previous one left, held fixed — which it does, to $10^{-15}$. Clipping is
checked against its definition.

**Agreement.** C and Python train the echo network twice — full backpropagation through time, then
Elman's truncation, from the same weights on the same batches — for 300 Adam updates with clipping,
and print a nine-number fingerprint per network: test loss and accuracy, the last batch loss and
gradient norm, how often the clip fired, a checksum of all 404 parameters and three sentinel weights.
They agree **to every printed digit**, for both. Two more lines pin the C recurrent layer (initial
state, input gradients, truncation) and clipping to NumPy's.

**Two Python paths.** The vectorized library and the C-order mirror compute the same initial loss and
gradients for both twins to $10^{-14}$, and the same parameters after three Adam steps to
$5 \times 10^{-15}$.

## Toy → real: echo, counting, then Shakespeare

**The echo twins.** A stream of random symbols from `A B C D`; at every step the target is the
symbol read four steps earlier. A 16-unit network, a fresh batch of 32 sequences of 24 steps per
update, Adam with clipping, 300 updates — trained twice from the same weights. With full
backpropagation through time its batch loss falls from 1.44 to 0.007, and it is perfect on test
sequences of 96 steps, four times longer than any it trained on: the same weights do the same thing
at every step, so the rule carries over. Elman's twin, whose gradient stops at the previous state,
ends at 43% (chance is 25%); its loss falls from 1.44 to 1.05 in the first 75 updates, then climbs
back to 1.24.
The forward passes are identical. What the Elman twin lacks is the signal that says "you should
have kept that symbol" — a signal that has to travel four steps back to reach the moment of keeping.

**The staircase.** Cut the gradient every $k$ steps and train each delay $d$ for 1,500 updates:

| gradient reaches back | d = 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
|-----------------------|------:|--:|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| 1 (Elman) | 0.27 | 0.27 | 0.26 | 0.29 | 0.32 | 0.28 |
| 2 | **1.00** | 0.27 | 0.28 | 0.27 | 0.27 | 0.26 |
| 4 | **1.00** | **1.00** | **1.00** | 0.30 | 0.26 | 0.26 |
| 8 | **1.00** | **1.00** | **1.00** | **1.00** | **1.00** | 0.26 |
| all | **1.00** | **1.00** | **1.00** | **1.00** | **1.00** | **1.00** |

Every delay shorter than the reach is learned perfectly; every other one stays at chance — not
partially learned, not learned slowly, not learned at all. Nothing else differs between the cells.
**A recurrent network learns to remember only as far back as its training gradient reaches.**

**The limit without truncation.** With the full gradient, on sequences of $2d + 16$ steps for 3,000
updates, the 16-unit network echoes perfectly at 4 and 8 steps, 99.6% at 12, 84% at 16, 59% at 20,
and nothing from 24 on. Part of that ceiling may be capacity — twenty two-bit symbols is a lot for
sixteen tanh units — but the next measurement shows the gradient running out on its own.

**The gradient through time.** Send a unit gradient into the last hidden state of a 40-step
sequence and measure how much reaches each earlier step, at initialization, with $W_{hh}$ drawn
Xavier-style and scaled by a gain:

| gain | spectral radius | 10 steps back | 39 steps back |
|-----:|----------------:|--------------:|--------------:|
| 0.5 | 0.52 | $1.2 \times 10^{-4}$ | $9 \times 10^{-16}$ |
| 1.0 (Xavier) | 1.03 | 0.065 | $1.9 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| 1.5 | 1.55 | 0.76 | 0.43 |
| 2.0 | 2.06 | 2.1 | 50 |
| 3.0 | 3.10 | 8.0 | 13,700 |

Below a spectral radius of about 1 the gradient vanishes geometrically; above it, it explodes. At
Xavier's own scale it falls about tenfold every eight steps, so at the start of training a 24-step
dependency sends back a signal a few thousandths the size of a 4-step one, buried under everything
nearer. Training reshapes the profile: the network trained on a 12-step delay holds its gradient
near 1 for twelve steps (0.998 at $k = 12$, against 0.037 at initialization) and then lets it fall to
0.27 by sixteen. It learned to keep the gradient alive for as long as its task needed — but only
as far as it could already see.

**The cliff.** The exploding half, reproduced with Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio's one-unit network:
$h^{\langle t\rangle} = \tanh(w\,h^{\langle t-1\rangle} + b)$ for 50 steps from $h = 0.5$, loss $\frac12 (h^{\langle 50\rangle} + 0.5)^2$.
Its loss surface over $(w, b)$ has a sharp diagonal ridge where the final state switches between the
map's two fixed points. Gradient descent from $(w, b) = (2, 0.5)$ walks onto the ridge, receives a
gradient hundreds of times larger than before, and takes a single step 27 units long that throws it
to $(-9, -25)$, where the state is pinned and the loss is stuck at 0.125. The same descent with the
gradient norm clipped at 1 crosses the ridge in steps of 0.2 and ends at a loss of $10^{-9}$. (On the
echo toy and on Shakespeare, trained with Adam, the clip never fires: Adam already divides each step
by a running size of the gradient.)

**Counting.** The second toy reads a stream of random bits and must output, at every step, the
number of 1s so far modulo 3. Trained on streams of 20 bits, the network is 100% correct on streams
of 1,000 and 10,000 — and so is the same network trained with Elman's one-step truncation. Its
16-dimensional hidden states fall into tight, separate clusters, each belonging to exactly one
count: a finite-state machine, built out of tanh units as attractors of the dynamics, which is why
it never drifts. It is not the minimal one. There are about a dozen clusters — the count *and the
last two bits read*, which together account for over 95% of the state's variance, against about a
quarter for the count alone. Nothing asked the network to be economical.
Why can Elman's training learn a counter but not a one-step echo? In the counter **every step's
target is the state itself**, so a one-step gradient can teach the update rule one step at a time.
In the echo, what must be kept is not what is asked for now; it is asked for $d$ steps later, and
only a gradient that travels $d$ steps back can say so. What matters is not how long the sequence
is, but how far apart a piece of information and the moment it is needed are.

**Tiny Shakespeare.** Then the real thing: 1,003,854 characters of training text, 111,540 of
validation (the last 10%, unseen passages), 65 distinct characters. The baselines are n-gram models,

the validation text to flatter them:

|-------|------------------------------:|

| bigram | 2.482 |

and about ten minutes of NumPy — reaches **1.654 nats per character** (2.39 bits), below every n-gram.

The order is the one Karpathy described: first letter frequencies and spaces, then the *form* of a
play — a capitalized name, a colon, a line break, short lines of verse — then mostly real words and
the occasional real phrase. The meaning never arrives. Lower the temperature to 0.3 and the model
plays safe and repeats itself ("the world think of bearth the world shall be…"); raise it to 1.2 and
it coins words ("exils profom-sour 'twom out norcaly").

**Elman's word boundaries, redone.** Measure the model's surprisal — the loss it pays — for every
validation character, and average it by the character's position inside its word:

| position | first letter | 2nd | 3rd | 4th | 5th | 6th+ | not a letter |
|----------|-------------:|----:|----:|----:|----:|-----:|-------------:|
| surprisal (nats) | **2.96** | 1.54 | 1.59 | 1.44 | 1.41 | 1.39 | 1.04 |

The first letter of a word costs about twice what any later letter does. Along a passage, the word
onsets stand up like the error peaks in Elman's figure: once a word has begun, spelling constrains
the rest of it; which word comes next is constrained only by meaning, and that is where most of the
model's remaining uncertainty lives.

**How far back does the gradient need to reach in text?** Train the same model for three epochs with
its gradient cut every $k$ characters inside each 64-character chunk: $k = 1$ (Elman) ends at 1.994
nats, $k = 4$ at 1.940, $k = 16$ at 1.967 and no cut at 1.969 — a spread of 0.05, about the size of the
difference between two seeds. It is the counter's lesson on real data. Next-character prediction is
supervised at every step, and most of what predicts the next character — the current word's spelling,
the last few characters — is a step or two back, where any gradient reaches. The long-range part of
language is a small share of the loss, and it is exactly the part a plain recurrent network
struggles to learn.

The [notebook](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/06-rnn/notebook.ipynb)
closes with the frameworks. PyTorch checks strictly: `torch.nn.RNN` given our weights returns our
hidden states and all five gradients to $10^{-14}$ (it keeps *two* biases, a cuDNN convention — they
are always added together, so each receives the same gradient); our truncation equals autograd with
`.detach()` every $k$ steps; and `clip_grad_norm_` matches ours, except that it divides by
$\|g\| + 10^{-6}$ and so lands a hair under the threshold. TensorFlow's `SimpleRNN`, trained with a
`GradientTape` and `tf.clip_by_global_norm`, echoes four steps back on 100% of test positions. Keras
builds the character model in six lines — an `Embedding` (the one-hot product as a lookup), a
`SimpleRNN(256, return_sequences=True)` and a `Dense` read-out — and reaches 1.830 nats in four
epochs of stateless 64-character windows, below the trigram. One Keras detail is worth remembering:
`global_clipnorm` is Pascanu's rule; its neighbour `clipnorm` clips each weight matrix separately.

## What it couldn't do

The echo toy draws the boundary sharply. A plain recurrent network learns any dependency its
training gradient can reach and carry back with enough signal left — and the gradient through time
is a power of a matrix, so "enough" runs out at a distance set by the spectral radius, not by the
amount of data or training. On Shakespeare the same limit is visible in the samples: a line holds
together, a speaker's name is followed by a colon and verse, but nothing survives from one speech to
the next. The model knows what the last few dozen characters looked like, not what the scene is about.

Bengio, Simard and Frasconi's theorem says that tuning will not fix this. A recurrent network whose
memories are robust to small perturbations has, necessarily, a contracting Jacobian through time —
and a contracting Jacobian is a vanishing gradient. The fix has to change the architecture.

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's fix, in 1997, was to give the network a second kind of state: a memory
cell that is not squashed through a tanh and multiplied by a weight matrix at every step, but *added
to*. Its update is $c^{\langle t\rangle} = f \odot c^{\langle t-1\rangle} + i \odot g$, with learned gates deciding what to
forget, what to write and what to read. Along $c$, the gradient is multiplied at each step by the
forget gate — a number the network can hold near one — instead of by $W_{hh}^{\top}$ and a tanh slope. They
called it the *constant error carousel*. It is a shortcut through time, the same idea as Module 05's
shortcut through depth, eighteen years earlier.

## Exercises & what's next

1. **Elman's temporal XOR.** Feed a bit stream in which every third bit is the XOR of the two before
   it, and train the network to predict the next bit, with `truncate=1` and with full BPTT. Plot the
   loss by position modulo 3. Which positions become predictable, under which training — and how does
   that square with §6's rule about what a one-step gradient can teach?
2. **Clip or not, with SGD.** Train the echo toy with plain SGD and momentum at a high learning rate,
   with and without `clip_grad_norm`, at delays 4 and 12. Log the gradient norm at every step. How
   often does it spike, and what happens to the loss on the step after a spike?
3. **Start at the identity.** Le, Jaitly and Hinton (2015) initialized a *ReLU* recurrent network with
   $W_{hh} = I$ and $b_h = 0$, so that at the start it copies its state forward unchanged. Implement the
   ReLU variant of `RNN` (forward and backward) and rerun the delay sweep. How far does the limit move,
   and what does the gradient-through-time profile look like at initialization?
4. **Orthogonal recurrence.** Draw $W_{hh}$ as a random orthogonal matrix (every eigenvalue on the unit
   circle) instead of Xavier's. Measure the gradient through time at initialization and after training
   on d = 16. Why does it not stay flat once training starts?
5. **Greedy decoding.** Sample from the Shakespeare model at temperature 0.05 (nearly argmax). What
   happens after a few lines, and why does a deterministic process with a finite state *have* to end
   up doing that?
6. **The cost of the loop.** Time one training update of the character model for $T$ = 16, 64 and 256
   at a fixed number of characters per update. Which part of the time scales with $T$ and cannot be
   batched away?

A recurrent network can carry anything forward in its state; what it cannot do is *learn* to carry
something far, because the lesson has to travel back the same distance and fades on the way.
