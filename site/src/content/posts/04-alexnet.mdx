## Ten points

On September 30, 2012, at eleven at night Greenwich time, the submission window for the third
ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge closed. Two weeks later, at a workshop in
Florence, the organizers put the classification results on a slide. The task was brutal: look at
a photograph — any of 100,000 test images, drawn from a thousand categories that included 120
breeds of dog — and name what is in it, with five guesses allowed. The previous two years'
winners had missed on 28.2% and then 25.8% of the images: hand-engineered features fed to
support vector machines, the polished state of the art, improving by a couple of points a year.

In 2012 the second-best team missed 26.2%. The best team, a group from the University of Toronto
calling itself SuperVision, missed **15.3%**.

It is hard to overstate how strange that number looked. Benchmarks move by fractions of a point;
a field's best ideas, compounding for a decade, had moved this one by a few points. Here was a
single entry, ten points clear of everybody — and it came from a technique most of the
computer-vision community had written off: a neural network, trained end to end from raw pixels,
exactly the kind of model Module 03 built for digits in 1998. The paper describing it —
*ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks*, by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya
Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton — appeared at NIPS that December, and within two years the
competition's leading entries were almost all convolutional networks. Within three, the systems
built on them were beating a trained human annotator at the same task.

This chapter is about that network, universally called **AlexNet**, and it has an unusual shape
for a chapter in this book. The core mechanism is not new — it is Module 03's convolution,
pooling and backpropagation, unchanged. What is new is everything around it: a network
**large** enough to see photographs, data large enough to feed it, hardware fast enough to train
it, and a set of tricks to stop sixty million parameters from simply memorizing a million
images. Those tricks — **ReLU** at depth, **dropout**, **data augmentation** — are the real
subject, because they are the part that survived. Every network in the rest of this book uses
some descendant of them.

## Two exponentials and a dataset

Module 03 ended in 1998, with LeNet-5 reading checks while the rest of machine learning drifted
toward support vector machines. Three things changed in the next fourteen years, and AlexNet is
what happened when they finally met.

**The data.** In 2006, Fei-Fei Li, then a young professor at the University of Illinois and soon
at Princeton, began arguing something unfashionable: that computer vision's bottleneck was not
the algorithms but the *data*. Benchmarks of the day had tens of categories and thousands of
images; she wanted every noun in WordNet that could be pictured, with hundreds of photos each.
Labeling that by hand was impossible for a lab, so ImageNet was labeled by the crowd: from 2008 to
2010 roughly 49,000 workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk, in 167 countries, sorted over 160
million candidate images scraped from search engines. ImageNet was presented, as a poster, at
CVPR 2009 — to modest interest. From 2010 its annual challenge used a 1,000-category subset:
**1.2 million training images**, the first labeled dataset big enough that a model with millions
of parameters had a chance of not just memorizing it.

**The hardware.** Video games had spent the 2000s funding a strange processor: thousands of
small arithmetic units built to do the same simple operation on many pixels at once. In 2007
NVIDIA's CUDA made those graphics cards programmable for general computation, and a matrix
multiply — which, as Module 03 showed, is what a convolution becomes — runs on one dozens of
times faster than on a CPU. Researchers had been running neural networks on graphics cards since
the mid-2000s; by 2011 Dan
Cireșan and colleagues in Jürgen Schmidhuber's lab at IDSIA were winning image competitions with
GPU-trained convolutional nets, including a traffic-sign contest where their system beat human
volunteers. Alex Krizhevsky, a graduate student of Hinton's, had written his own unusually fast
CUDA convolution code, cuda-convnet, and used it on CIFAR-10 — a small 32×32 image dataset he
had assembled in 2009, and the one this chapter trains on.

**The tricks.** Hinton's group had spent the late 2000s on the problem of training deep networks
at all, and by 2012 had two new tools that would matter: the **rectified linear unit**
(Nair & Hinton, 2010) and **dropout** (Hinton et al., posted to arXiv in July 2012).

In the usual telling it was Sutskever who was convinced that a big enough convolutional
network on ImageNet would win, and Krizhevsky who made it run: a network split across two NVIDIA
GTX 580 gaming cards with 3 GB of memory each, trained for roughly ninety passes over the 1.2
million images — five to six days, in Krizhevsky's bedroom at his parents' house, as the story is
usually told. The network had about **60 million parameters**. LeNet-5 had 60 thousand.

The aftermath moved fast. At NIPS that December, in a Lake Tahoe casino hotel, the three authors'
newly formed company — no products, three employees — was auctioned between Google, Microsoft,
Baidu and DeepMind; by Cade Metz's account Google won at $44 million. Nearly every leading entry
in 2013 was a convolutional net; by 2015 the error had fallen to 3.6%, below the roughly 5%
measured for a trained human annotator. Computer vision changed its mind in about one conference cycle.

| Year | Milestone | What it added |
|------|-----------|---------------|
| 2009 | Deng, Li et al., *ImageNet* (CVPR) | a labeled dataset big enough to feed millions of parameters |
| 2009 | Krizhevsky, CIFAR-10 | the small-image testbed (and cuda-convnet's proving ground) |
| 2010 | Nair & Hinton, ReLU | the non-saturating unit that makes depth trainable |
| 2011 | Cireșan et al., GPU CNNs | convolutional nets winning contests on graphics cards |
| 2012 | Hinton et al., dropout | a regularizer for networks far larger than their data |
| 2012 | Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton | all of it at once: 15.3% vs 26.2% on ImageNet |

## The intuition: big enough to see, restrained enough to generalize

Here is the problem AlexNet solved, in one sentence: **a network large enough to recognize a
thousand kinds of objects is large enough to memorize every training image instead — so you need
a way to give it capacity without letting it cheat.**

The capacity part is LeNet scaled up, and nothing about it is conceptually new. Photographs are
224 pixels across, not 28, so the network needs more layers to grow its receptive fields to
object size, and more filters per layer, because a thousand categories need many more kinds of
evidence than ten digits. Five convolutional layers, three dense layers, 650,000 neurons. Two
small changes to the convolution keep the cost survivable. A **stride** — sliding the kernel four
pixels at a time in the first layer instead of one — computes a sixteenth of the positions at the
layer where the image is largest. **Overlapping pooling** — 3×3 windows every 2 pixels — summarizes
a little more smoothly than Module 03's disjoint tiles.

The trouble is the arithmetic of memorization. Sixty million adjustable numbers against 1.2
million training images is about fifty parameters per image. A network that big can, in
principle, simply store its answers: shape its decision boundary around every individual
training photo and learn nothing that transfers to a new one. Every model with more parameters
than constraints faces this; Module 02 met the mild version as overfitting. AlexNet's version was
severe, and its answer came in three layers.

**First, make depth trainable at all — ReLU.** A sigmoid or tanh unit squashes: for large inputs
its slope goes to zero, and backpropagation multiplies one slope per layer, so the gradient
reaching the early layers of a deep tanh network is a product of many small numbers. ReLU's slope
is exactly 1 wherever the unit is on. Gradients pass through active units at full strength, and
in the paper's measurement a ReLU network trained six times faster than the same network with
tanh. (Our own attempt to reproduce that measurement, later in this chapter, holds a surprise.)
This is not regularization — it is what makes the big network worth having.

**Second, manufacture more data — augmentation.** A photo of a dog, shifted a few pixels or
mirrored left to right, is still a photo of a dog. So train on random crops and mirror images,
drawn fresh every pass: each original becomes thousands of slightly different, honestly labeled
examples. This is the cheapest regularizer there is, and the most principled, because it tells
the network something *true*: these variations do not change the answer.

**Third, forbid conspiracies — dropout.** During training, silence every hidden unit in the dense
layers with probability ½, independently, at every step. Each step therefore trains a different
random sub-network, and no unit can rely on any particular partner being present to fix its
mistakes. The brittle, co-adapted teamwork that memorization depends on — this unit fires only
for that training image, because those three other units cover the rest — stops working.
Hinton's analogy was sexual reproduction: genes that must work with random partners evolve to be
individually useful. A more operational view: a network with $n$ droppable units contains $2^n$
thinned sub-networks sharing one set of weights, and dropout trains a random one at every step —
an ensemble too large to enumerate, averaged for free at test time.

Augmentation and dropout attack overfitting from opposite ends. Augmentation adds *information*:
it widens what the data shows. Dropout adds *restraint*: it narrows what the network can do with
whatever data it has. As the toy in this chapter shows, they are not interchangeable.

And they went exactly where they were needed. Count AlexNet's parameters and **96% of them** sit
in the three dense layers at the top — fc6 alone, connecting a 6×6×256 feature map to 4,096
units, holds 38 million. Count its arithmetic and **92% of it** is in the five convolutions. So
dropout went on the dense layers, where the memorizing capacity lived, and the GPUs went to the
convolutions, where the work was.

## The math: strides, padding, overlap, and a random mask

Notation as in Module 03: images $X$ of shape $(n, C, H, W)$, kernels $K$ of shape
$(F, C, K_H, K_W)$, output positions $(u, v)$, kernel taps $(p, q)$.

### Stride and padding

Module 03 slid its kernel one pixel at a time and only where it fit. Generalize both. With a
**stride** $S$ the window's top-left corner moves $S$ pixels per step; with **padding**
$P_{\text{pad}}$ the input is surrounded by that many rings of zeros, so windows can center on
border pixels. Output cell $(u, v)$ now reads the padded input starting at $(uS, vS)$:

$$
Y[i, f, u, v] \;=\; b_f \;+\; \sum_{c, p, q} K[f, c, p, q]\;
X[i,\, c,\, uS + p - P_{\text{pad}},\, vS + q - P_{\text{pad}}],
$$

where any tap that lands in the padding reads zero and simply drops out of the sum. Counting the
valid window positions along one axis gives the formula Module 03 promised:

$$
H_{\text{out}} \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{H + 2P_{\text{pad}} - K_H}{S} \right\rfloor + 1 .
$$

Run AlexNet's first layer through it: 11×11 kernels at stride 4, no padding. On the 224×224 input
the paper describes, $\lfloor (224 - 11)/4 \rfloor + 1 = 54$ — but the paper's own figure says
55, and $(227 - 11)/4 + 1 = 55$ exactly. The network really ran on 227×227 crops; "224" is one of
deep learning's most famous typos, and the formula is how you catch it. At stride 1 the same
layer would compute $217^2 = 47{,}089$ positions per filter instead of $55^2 = 3{,}025$ — the
stride buys a factor of 15 at the single most expensive layer, at the price of a coarser map.

The backward pass changes only in its indexing. Each output cell still multiplied one kernel
weight by one pixel per tap, so the chain rule still sends each $dY$ back to the weight times the
pixel and to the pixel times the weight:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial K[f,c,p,q]} = \sum_{i,u,v} dY[i,f,u,v]\; X[i, c, uS{+}p{-}P_{\text{pad}}, vS{+}q{-}P_{\text{pad}}],
$$

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_f} = \sum_{i,u,v} dY[i,f,u,v],
$$

and $\partial L / \partial X$ collects, for every pixel, the gradients of the output cells whose
windows covered it, each times the kernel tap that connected them. Gradient landing on a padding
zero has nowhere to go and is dropped — padding was never an input.

### Overlapping pooling

The same window formula governs pooling. Module 03 used $P = S = 2$: disjoint tiles. AlexNet used
$P = 3,\ S = 2$: each window shares a row or column with its neighbors, and a cell in the overlap
can be the maximum of **two** windows (in two dimensions, up to four). The forward pass doesn't
care. The backward pass must: a value used twice in the forward pass receives the sum of both
gradients — the chain rule's rule for any shared variable — so the routing of Module 03 becomes a
scatter-*add*:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial X[i,c,h,w]} \;=\; \sum_{\text{windows }(u,v)\text{ won by }(h,w)} dY[i,c,u,v].
$$

The paper reported overlapping pooling lowered error by 0.3–0.4 points and made the network
"slightly more difficult to overfit". It is a minor trick; the bug it invites, as the assignment
shows, is not.

### ReLU, at depth

For a chain of layers $a^{(\ell)} = \phi(z^{(\ell)})$, backpropagation multiplies the incoming
gradient by $\phi'(z^{(\ell)})$ at every layer. For tanh,

$$
\tanh'(z) = 1 - \tanh^2(z) \;\le\; 1, \qquad \tanh'(\pm 2) \approx 0.07,
$$

so eight layers of moderately large pre-activations shrink the gradient by orders of magnitude.
For ReLU, $\phi'(z) = [z > 0]$: exactly 1 on active units. The gradient reaching layer one is
not attenuated by the activations at all — only redirected by which units are on. (ReLU has its
own failure, units that never turn on again; Module 02's He initialization is the matching fix.)

### Dropout

Let $h$ be a hidden layer's activations and $p$ the drop probability. During training draw one
uniform number per unit and form the **mask**

$$
m_j \;=\; \frac{[\,u_j < 1 - p\,]}{1 - p}, \qquad u_j \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1), \qquad
\tilde h = h \odot m .
$$

Each unit survives with probability $1-p$; survivors are scaled up by $1/(1-p)$ so that
$\mathbb{E}[\tilde h_j] = h_j$ — the expected activation feeding the next layer is unchanged. At
test time nothing is dropped and nothing is scaled: the full network, with every unit, is exactly
the expected thinned network. This is **inverted** dropout, the form every framework uses; the
2012 paper did the bookkeeping the other way round (no scaling in training, outputs halved at
test), which gives the same expectation.

The backward pass is one line. A dropped unit contributed nothing to the forward pass, so it gets
nothing back; a survivor's gradient is scaled by the same factor its activation was:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h} \;=\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial \tilde h} \odot m .
$$

It is ReLU's gate again — a 0/1 switch the forward pass recorded — except that the switch is
thrown by a random number instead of by the sign of $z$.

### Augmentation

AlexNet trained on random 224×224 crops of 256×256 images, each mirrored left-right with
probability ½: $32 \times 32$ crop positions times 2 mirrors, the paper's "factor of 2048" more
training data. At test time it averaged the softmax over ten fixed views — the four corner crops
and the center crop, each plain and mirrored. It added one more invariance: a random shift of
every pixel's color along the principal components of the training set's RGB values (*PCA color
augmentation*), because a photo under warmer light shows the same object. Crops and mirrors cost
a few lines of indexing. Their mathematics is the statement that the label is unchanged by them.

## The code: the same chain rule, with offsets and a random mask

Convolution and pooling grow their stride and padding in both libraries, and Module 03's
functions become the special case. In C
([`lib/c/nanograd/conv.c`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/lib/c/nanograd/conv.c)),
the forward pass is Module 03's seven loops with two new terms in the index and one bounds check:

```c
for (int c = 0; c < C; c++)
    for (int p = 0; p < KH; p++)
        for (int q = 0; q < KW; q++) {
            const int h = u * S + p - pad, w = v * S + q - pad;
            if (h < 0 || h >= H || w < 0 || w >= W)
                continue;            /* a padding zero: adds nothing */
            acc += K[((f * C + c) * KH + p) * KW + q]
                 * X[((i * C + c) * H + h) * W + w];
        }
```

and `ng_conv2d_forward` — Module 03's call — is now one line that passes `S = 1, pad = 0`. With
no tap ever out of bounds, Module 03's agreement test still produces the same bits. The NumPy
im2col version strides its slices (`X[:, :, p:p + S*OH:S, q:q + S*OW:S]`) and pads with `np.pad`;
its adjoint, col2im, scatters back into a padded buffer and crops the padding off.

Max pooling's backward was already a scatter, so overlap costs nothing in C — the `+=` was there
all along:

```c
for (int o = 0; o < n * C * OH * OW; o++)
    dX[idx[o]] += dY[o];       /* a cell that won two windows gets both */
```

In NumPy the same line is a trap. `dX[idx] += dY` with repeated indices does *not* accumulate —
NumPy writes each repeated index once and silently discards the rest — which is exactly the
overlap case. `np.add.at(dX, idx, dY)` accumulates. The assignment makes you meet this bug on
purpose, because the finite-difference check only catches it when a test input happens to make
one cell win two windows.

Dropout in C is a mask and two loops
([`lib/c/nanograd/nn.c`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/lib/c/nanograd/nn.c)):

```c
const double keep = 1.0 - p;
for (int j = 0; j < n; j++) {
    mask[j] = (ng_rng_uniform(r) < keep) ? 1.0 / keep : 0.0;
    Y[j] = X[j] * mask[j];
}
/* backward: dX[j] = dY[j] * mask[j] */
```

The randomness is the interesting engineering. The C and Python implementations must agree to the
last bit, so the masks — and the crop offsets, and the mirror coin-flips — must be the same random
numbers in the same order. Both libraries draw them from the book's 64-bit LCG, each from its own
stream (separate seeds for data, weights, augmentation and dropout, so that changing one can't
shift another's numbers). The Python `Dropout` and `random_crop_flip` call `rng.random(shape)`,
which a fast NumPy generator answers for CIFAR and the LCG answers, element by element, for the
agreement test. One more library change makes dropout usable: networks now have modes.
`net.train()` turns dropout on; `net.eval()` turns it off before measuring anything.

### The toy program, end to end

[`c/alexnet.c`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/04-alexnet/c/alexnet.c)
is the whole 2012 recipe at toy scale — augment, strided conv, overlapping pool, dropout, momentum
SGD with weight decay:

```c
ng_random_crop_flip(Xtr, XC, N, 3, 12, 12, 10, 1, &aug_rng);   /* fresh views */
ng_conv2d_forward_strided(XC, K1, b1, Z, N, 3, 10, 10, 6, 5, 5, 2, 2);
ng_relu_forward(Z, A, N * 6 * 5 * 5);
ng_maxpool2d_forward_strided(A, POOLED, IDX, N, 6, 5, 5, 3, 2);
ng_linear_forward(POOLED, W2, b2, H_PRE, N, 24, 16);
ng_relu_forward(H_PRE, H, N * 16);
ng_dropout_forward(H, D, MASK, N * 16, 0.5, &drop_rng);        /* a new thinned net */
ng_linear_forward(D, W3, b3, LOGITS, N, 16, 2);
/* ...softmax cross-entropy, then the reversed walk: */
ng_dropout_backward(MASK, DD, DH, N * 16);
ng_maxpool2d_backward_strided(IDX, DPOOL, DA, N, 6, 5, 5, 3, 2);
ng_conv2d_backward_strided(XC, K1, DZ, NULL, gK1, gb1, N, 3, 10, 10, 6, 5, 5, 2, 2);
/* SGD: v = 0.9 v + (g + 5e-4 w);  w -= lr v   -- AlexNet's update rule */
```

Backpropagation through dropout and overlapping pooling is still the reversed walk of Module 01:
dropout gates the gradient like ReLU, pooling routes and now adds, the strided convolution scatters
through its offsets. The Python mirror has the same split as Module 03: a scalar `ToyAlexNet` that
walks the C's loops in the C's order, and the identical network assembled from vectorized
`nanograd` layers, pinned to each other by a test.

For CIFAR the vectorized path drops to **float32** — `net.astype(np.float32)` re-stores every
parameter, and everything downstream follows the dtype of its inputs. The teaching code has run in
float64 since Module 00 because the agreement tests need it; at CIFAR scale half the bytes is
roughly half the time, and AlexNet itself trained in single precision. The toy stays in float64.

## The gradient check, and C↔Python agreement

[`tests/test_agreement.py`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/04-alexnet/tests/test_agreement.py)
holds the three gates every module in this book has.

**Gradients.** The strided, padded convolution is finite-difference checked at three
stride/padding combinations ($dK$, $db$, $dX$, all near `1e-10`) and pinned to the naive
definition at `1e-15`; overlapping max-pooling, dropout (with its mask held fixed), local response
normalization, and the full toy network end-to-end through softmax cross-entropy all match their
numerical gradients.

**Agreement.** C and Python train the toy for 600 steps — 600 batches of random crops and mirrors,
600 dropout masks, momentum and weight decay — and print an eight-number fingerprint: final loss,
train and test accuracy, a checksum of all 890 parameters, four sentinel weights. They agree **to
every printed digit**. A second line checks the C local response normalization's forward and
backward against NumPy's, also to every digit. This is the strongest agreement so far, and it is
not luck: every random number comes from the same stream, every sum runs in the same order, and
the only floating-point operations that could differ between the two math libraries (`exp`,
`pow`) happen to round identically here.

**Two Python paths.** The vectorized network and the scalar mirror must compute the same initial
loss and, after three full training steps with the LCG's crops and masks, the same parameters
(they agree to `1e-13`).

Dropout is easiest to understand by watching a network memorize and then watching it fail to.
Below, a wide two-hidden-layer network — over 4,000 weights — trains on just forty points, some

Start with **p = 0** and press Play. Training accuracy reaches 100% within a few hundred steps —
the network puts every training point on its own side of the boundary, including the mislabeled
ones, by bending the boundary into narrow fingers and pockets around them. The dashed curve,
accuracy on 600 fresh points from the same distribution, flattens around 75% and stays there:
the extra effort went into points that were wrong to begin with, and fresh points that land in
those pockets pay for it. Now pick **p = 0.5**. The grids at top right show the thinned network
of each step — half the units gone, a different half every time. A pocket built around one
mislabeled point needs a specific team of units, and the team is never all present, so the
network fits it later and less firmly: training accuracy climbs more slowly and stops short of
100%, and accuracy on fresh points keeps creeping up, ending around 80%. The difference in the
picture is subtle — the measured gap is not. Then try **p = 0.75**: too much restraint, and test
accuracy ends below even p = 0's. Dropout is a dial, not a switch.

## Toy → real: mirror glyphs, then CIFAR-10

The toy is built to isolate AlexNet's problem. Each 12×12 color image holds one small corner
shape — a vertical stroke joined to a horizontal stroke at the **bottom** (class 0) or the **top**
(class 1) — in a random color, at a random position, over noise. The label is about up versus
down, so mirroring never changes it. The catch: all 32 training images have their vertical
stroke on the left, and every test image is **mirrored**. A network that memorizes its training
set has never seen a test image's shape.

The network is AlexNet in miniature: a 10×10 crop, a 5×5 convolution with stride 2 and padding 2,
ReLU, 3×3 overlapping max-pooling with stride 2, a 16-unit dense layer with dropout, and a
two-way output — 890 parameters, trained for 600 steps of SGD with momentum and weight decay. The
canonical run (the one C and Python agree on) memorizes all 32 training glyphs and classifies
**92% of the mirrored test glyphs** correctly.

The notebook then asks *which* trick did that, over ten random seeds:

| configuration | train accuracy | mirrored test accuracy |
|---------------|---------------:|-----------------------:|
| nothing | 100% | 69% |
| dropout only | 100% | 68% |
| crops + mirrors only | 100% | **96%** |
| crops + mirrors + dropout | 98% | 93% |

Every configuration memorizes the training set. Only the ones that see mirrored crops recognize
mirrored glyphs — and dropout, on its own, does nothing for them. That is the most useful result
in this chapter, so it is worth saying plainly: **regularizers are not interchangeable.**
Augmentation added information the data didn't contain — "a mirrored glyph is the same glyph" —
and no amount of restraint can conjure information. Dropout's job is different: making a network
with far more parameters than examples behave as if it had fewer. In this toy the 890 parameters
are not the problem; the missing mirror images are. In AlexNet, with sixty million parameters
against a million images, both problems were real.

Then CIFAR-10. Our network keeps AlexNet's silhouette — five convolutions (the first two followed
by overlapping pooling, three stacked, one more pool), three dense layers with dropout on the two
hidden ones, ReLU everywhere — sized for 24×24 crops of 32×32 photos, Krizhevsky's own CIFAR
recipe, and a laptop CPU:

```
24×24×3 crop
conv1 32@5×5 pad 2 → ReLU → maxpool 3/2        24 → 11
conv2 64@3×3 pad 1 → ReLU → maxpool 3/2        11 → 5
conv3 96@3×3 → conv4 96@3×3 → conv5 64@3×3 → maxpool 3/2     5 → 2
fc6 256 → dropout → fc7 256 → dropout → fc8 10        348,874 parameters
```

It trains with one honest departure from 2012. AlexNet used SGD with momentum and a learning rate
cut by hand over some ninety epochs. On our budget of fifteen, a plain eight-layer network
without batch normalization (Module 05's subject) learns too slowly under SGD — after ten epochs on
the same data the notebook measures 44% training error with SGD and momentum against 28% with
Adam — so the CIFAR network uses Adam (Module 02), with the learning rate cut tenfold twice near
the end. The toy keeps AlexNet's SGD
recipe exactly. Everything else is the 2012 recipe: random crops and mirrors, dropout on fc6 and
fc7, ReLU, float32. The full run — all 50,000 images, fifteen epochs — took about two hours of
NumPy on a laptop CPU, which is why the notebook caches it:

| epoch | train loss | test accuracy |
|-------|-----------:|--------------:|
| 1 | 1.960 | 41.75% |
| 3 | 1.430 | 57.31% |
| 5 | 1.231 | 62.08% |
| 10 | 1.037 | 67.88% |
| 11 (learning rate ÷ 10) | 0.928 | 71.45% |
| 13 | 0.885 | 72.54% |
| 15 (learning rate ÷ 100) | 0.870 | **72.33%** |

The verification gate asserts ≥ 70%, and the run clears it with **72.3%** — 2,767 of 10,000 test
photos missed. The jump at epoch 11 is the learning-rate cut doing exactly what AlexNet's
hand-dropped rates did: the noisy steps that were exploring settle into the basin they found.
AlexNet's ten-crop test-time averaging adds another 3.2 points on top, for **75.5%** — ten looks
at the same photo, averaged, are better than one.

The notebook reruns the paper's two key experiments on a 10,000-image subset. First, **Figure 1**:
the same network with no regularization of any kind, ReLU against tanh, SGD with momentum as in
the paper. **It did not reproduce.** With each activation given the initialization derived for it
in Module 02 (He for ReLU, Xavier for tanh) and the same learning rate, tanh reached 50% training
error at epoch 3 and ReLU only at epoch 8 — and a learning-rate sweep in both directions, which
the paper also did, left tanh ahead. We can't rerun 2012's exact experiment, but the setups
differ in ways that plausibly matter: theirs was a four-layer network with weights drawn at
hand-picked fixed scales, ours is eight layers with fan-in-scaled initialization, and the paper
itself warns that the effect "varies with network architecture". ReLU won the decade anyway, for
reasons this experiment doesn't probe — it costs a comparison instead of an exponential, which
mattered on 2012 GPUs, and its gradient is not attenuated by the activations at all, which matters
more the deeper a network gets. A famous figure is a measurement of a setup, and the setup is part
of the result.

Second, the regularizers one at a time, tracking clean training accuracy (evaluation mode, center
crops) against test accuracy over 30 epochs:

| configuration | train accuracy | test accuracy | gap |
|---------------|---------------:|--------------:|----:|
| nothing | 100.0% | 56.6% | 43.4 pts |
| dropout only | 98.9% | 57.2% | 41.7 pts |
| crops + mirrors only | 74.7% | **66.3%** | 8.4 pts |
| crops + mirrors + dropout | 68.4% | 62.8% | 5.6 pts |

The toy's lesson, at scale. Unregularized, the network memorizes every one of the 10,000 images
and its test accuracy stalls at 56.6%; dropout alone barely moves that. Crops and mirrors are the
big lever: ten points of test accuracy, and the memorization gap collapses from 43 points to 8.
Adding dropout on top shrinks the gap a little further but *costs* accuracy here — 62.8% against
66.3%. That is the honest shape of the trade-off: dropout buys generalization with capacity and
convergence time (the paper notes it "roughly doubles the number of iterations required to
converge"; at twelve epochs, both dropout runs here were still behind the unregularized one),
and in our network — whose dense layers hold only 38% of the parameters, against AlexNet's 96% —
over thirty epochs on 10,000 images, the price isn't worth paying. AlexNet, with sixty million
parameters almost all in fc6 and fc7, was the opposite case. Our canonical run keeps the 2012
recipe; whether dropout earns its keep at full scale is exercise 2.

And then it looks inside — and meets a small, honest disappointment. Krizhevsky's Figure 3, the
most reproduced picture of the network, shows 96 crisp first-layer filters: oriented edges and
blobs of color. Plot our 32 and they look like colored noise. Nothing is broken; the network just
hasn't trained long enough for its first layer to forget where it started. Over fifteen epochs on
50,000 small images, conv1's weights moved only **37%** of the way from their random
initialization (the deeper layers moved 65–115%), so the random start still dominates the
picture. Subtract it — plot what training *changed* — and the structure appears: smooth color
blobs and light-to-dark gradients, with neighboring kernel taps strongly correlated (0.36–0.51)
where the raw weights are not (≈ 0). AlexNet's crispness was bought with ninety epochs over 1.2
million images.

The paper's Figure 4 made a subtler point, and the notebook redoes it: take the last hidden
layer's activations as an image's *description*, and images with nearby descriptions look alike
to a person even when their pixels are far apart. Ours, with 256 numbers instead of 4,096, mostly
agrees: a white horse's nearest neighbors are five horses of every color and pose, a ship's are
ships (and one airplane, nose-on). Its one car query, a white car with its door open, finds white
fluffy dogs — and it is worth noticing that this is exactly the kind of confusion the paper's
far richer description avoided. That 4,096-number vector, it turned out, was useful far beyond
the thousand ImageNet classes; using it as a general-purpose image representation became, for a
few years, one of the most common starting points in all of computer vision.

The [notebook](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/04-alexnet/notebook.ipynb)
closes with the frameworks. PyTorch checks strictly: `F.conv2d(stride=2, padding=2)`,
`F.max_pool2d(3, 2)` and `F.local_response_norm` receive our exact inputs and gradients in
float64, and outputs and gradients match ours to `1e-13` or better — with one lesson about reading the
documentation: PyTorch's LRN divides $\alpha$ by the window size, so matching the paper means
passing $5\alpha$. The toy network's every parameter gradient matches autograd with our dropout
mask injected into the torch graph. TensorFlow (a `GradientTape` loop with `tf.image` crops) and
Keras (`RandomFlip` and `Dropout` as layers that switch themselves off at inference) train the
same architecture to 70.8% and 68.8%. One framework lesson came free: Keras's own
`RandomCrop` layer crops a whole batch at one location and *resizes* at inference instead of
center-cropping — a different augmentation from AlexNet's, so the crops live in the input pipeline
instead. Reading what a library layer actually does is part of using it.

## What AlexNet actually was

The real network was these mechanisms at full size, plus a few choices that history kept and a
few it didn't. Every one of them was a reasonable answer to a 2012 constraint.

**Two GPUs, by necessity.** Sixty million parameters and a training batch's activations did not
fit in one GTX 580's 3 GB. So the network was cut in half lengthwise: each GPU held half the
kernels of every layer, and the halves talked only at conv3 and at the dense layers. The paper
reported the split *helped* slightly over a single-GPU network with half the kernels — and found
something stranger in the filters: on every run, conv1's half on GPU 1 learned mostly
color-agnostic edges and the half on GPU 2 mostly color blobs. The architecture's wiring decided
what each half could specialize in. The split survives as **grouped convolution**, the direct
descendant of LeNet-5's connection table from Module 03 and the ancestor of the depthwise
convolutions in today's mobile networks.

**Local response normalization.** After the first two convolutions, every activation was divided
by a power of the energy of its neighbors across channels at the same pixel:

$$
b_c = a_c \Big/ \Big(k + \alpha \!\!\sum_{j=\max(0,\,c-n/2)}^{\min(N-1,\,c+n/2)}\!\! a_j^2\Big)^{\beta},
\qquad k = 2,\ n = 5,\ \alpha = 10^{-4},\ \beta = 0.75 ,
$$

modeled on *lateral inhibition* in real neurons — a strongly firing cell suppresses its
neighbors. The paper credited it with 1.2–1.4 points of error. Two years later VGG found it bought
nothing, and batch normalization (Module 05) replaced it entirely. We implement it in both
languages anyway — its backward pass, where every output depends on five inputs, is a good warm-up
for batch normalization's. And the notebook's ablation, at our small scale, does *not* side with
VGG: over twelve epochs on 10,000 images, adding LRN after conv1 and conv2 lifted test accuracy
from 51.4% to 53.7%. One seed on a small budget is a hint, not a verdict — VGG's comparison was on
ImageNet with much deeper networks — but it is a useful reminder that "obsolete" in deep learning
usually means *superseded*, not *useless*. LRN was reaching for something real: keeping
activation scales in check as signals pass through layers. Batch normalization does that job
directly, which is why it, and not LRN, is in every network from Module 05 on.

**Initialization and the schedule.** Weights from $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.01^2)$, a fixed standard
deviation chosen by hand a few years before He initialization (Module 02) derived it from the
fan-in. Biases set to **1** in conv2, conv4, conv5 and the hidden dense layers, so that ReLUs
started in their active region. A learning rate of 0.01, divided by ten by hand whenever the
validation error stopped improving — three times in total — over roughly ninety epochs.

**Ensembles and extra data.** The 15.3% that won was not one network. A single AlexNet scored
18.2% top-5 error on the validation set; averaging five of them gave 16.4%, and the winning entry
averaged seven, two of which had first been pre-trained on the larger 15-million-image Fall 2011
release of ImageNet. Even the single network beat the field by eight points; the ensemble is what
made the margin ten.

The pattern from Module 03 holds: AlexNet's dead ends were hand-tuned special cases that later
work replaced with general rules. The fixed $0.01$ became He initialization. The hand-dropped
learning rate became schedules and adaptive optimizers. LRN became batch normalization. The GPU
split became a design choice instead of a memory constraint. What did not get replaced — ReLU,
dropout, augmentation, depth trained end to end on big data with GPUs — is the part every chapter
from here on inherits.

## What it couldn't do

AlexNet's lesson was taken as *deeper is better*, and the field tested it immediately. A refinement
of AlexNet by Matthew Zeiler (with Rob Fergus) won the 2013 challenge; by 2014 Oxford's
VGG network replaced AlexNet's large first kernels with stacks of 3×3 convolutions — two 3×3
layers see a 5×5 region with fewer weights and one more nonlinearity — and went to **nineteen**
layers, roughly halving AlexNet's error. Google's entry that year went deeper still.

Then depth stopped working. Push a plain convolutional network past about twenty layers and its
accuracy *falls* — and not on the test set only, which would be overfitting, but on the
**training** set. A deeper network can always represent a shallower one (let the extra layers
compute the identity), so a deeper network that fits its own training data *worse* is not a
capacity problem or a regularization problem. It is an optimization problem: gradient descent
cannot find the solution that provably exists. ReLU had made gradients survive eight layers; at
thirty they were again getting lost on the way down.

The fix, in 2015, was a single line of arithmetic — add each block's input to its output — and it
let networks train at 152 layers. That is the next chapter.
