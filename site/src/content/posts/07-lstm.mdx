## The constant error carousel

The last chapter ended on a proof. In 1994 Yoshua Bengio, Patrice Simard and Paolo Frasconi showed
that a recurrent network which stores information *robustly* — so that small disturbances cannot
erase it — is exactly a network whose gradients from the distant past vanish. Learning long-range
dependencies by gradient descent was, in the title's word, difficult, and the difficulty was not a
matter of tuning.

Sepp Hochreiter had found the vanishing gradient three years earlier, in his diploma thesis at the
Technical University of Munich, supervised by Jürgen Schmidhuber. In 1997 the two of them published
the way around it, in a 46-page paper in *Neural Computation* called *Long Short-Term Memory*. The
argument starts from the arithmetic of the problem. The error flowing back through a recurrent unit
is multiplied at every step by the unit's weight onto itself times the slope of its activation. For
that product to stay at exactly one, step after step, the unit must be linear and its self-weight
must be 1.0. Such a unit's state never changes on its own — whatever it holds, it keeps, and the
error flowing back through it never shrinks or grows. They called it the **constant error carousel**.

A unit that keeps everything is also a unit that can be overwritten by anything. So they put
**gates** around it: multiplicative units, learned like any other, that decide when the cell may be
written to and when it may be read. The gated cell could learn to latch a symbol and hold it across
a thousand time steps of noise — a task on which, the paper reports, backpropagation through time
and real-time recurrent learning already failed at ten.

This chapter builds the LSTM in its modern form, with the forget gate that was added three years
later, and measures what the carousel buys: a memory the gradient can travel along, a toy where an
LSTM holds a symbol eight times further than Module 06's network ever does, and the one condition
that toy depends on — which the 1997 paper had already spelled out, and which the customary
initialization quietly drops.

## Memory that lasts, 1991–2016

The LSTM was a decade early. Its paper's experiments were small, its training algorithm was unusual,
and for years few people outside Schmidhuber's labs in Munich and Lugano used it.

The first version circulated as a technical report in 1995, and a short paper appeared at NIPS the
next year; the full paper came in 1997. Its cell had two gates, input and output, and no way to
forget: the carousel's self-weight was fixed at 1.0. In 1999 Felix Gers, working with Schmidhuber
and Fred Cummins at IDSIA, found what that costs on a stream that never ends — the cell's contents
grow without bound, because nothing ever clears them — and added a third gate, the **forget gate**,
that lets a cell reset itself (the journal version is from 2000). Gers and Schmidhuber then added
"peephole" connections from the cell to its gates, for tasks that need precise timing.

The 1997 paper had trained the cell with a deliberately truncated gradient: error was allowed to
flow back in time only along the carousels, never out through the gates. In 2005 Alex Graves and
Schmidhuber trained LSTMs with the full gradient through time, and bidirectionally, on speech. A
year later Graves and colleagues introduced connectionist temporal classification, a loss that lets
a recurrent network label an unsegmented sequence, and in 2009 LSTMs trained with it won
international competitions in handwriting recognition. In 2013 Graves, Abdel-rahman Mohamed and
Geoffrey Hinton stacked them into deep networks and set a record on the TIMIT speech benchmark;
the same year Graves had them generate text and handwriting.

Then the field caught up. In 2014 Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le translated English to
French with a four-layer LSTM (Module 09). In 2015 Rafal Jozefowicz, Wojciech Zaremba and Sutskever
searched ten thousand recurrent architectures for something better and found that the LSTM's
remaining gap closed once its forget gate's bias was set to 1; Klaus Greff and colleagues ablated
every part of the cell and found the forget gate and the output activation the parts that matter
most; Andrej Karpathy, Justin Johnson and Li Fei-Fei looked inside character-level LSTMs and found
cells that tracked quotation marks and line lengths. The same spring, Rupesh Srivastava, Greff and
Schmidhuber turned the gates sideways — *highway networks*, the LSTM's gated shortcut run through
depth instead of time — and in December ResNet (Module 05) showed the shortcut works without the
gates. By 2016 LSTMs were inside Google's speech recognition and, eight layers deep, its translation
system.

## The intuition: a ledger, not a rewrite

Module 06's network keeps its memory the way you might keep a summary of a meeting by rewriting it,
in full, after every sentence anyone says. Each rewrite runs the whole summary through the same
transformation, so anything that has to survive fifty rewrites must survive fifty applications of
the same matrix — and the blame for forgetting it has to travel back through all fifty.

The LSTM keeps a **ledger** instead. Each step, three clerks act on it. One decides how much of the
existing ledger to keep (the forget gate). One decides how much of a new entry to write (the input
gate). One decides how much of the ledger to read out to the rest of the network (the output gate).
The ledger itself is never transformed — only kept, added to, and read. If the keeping clerk says
"keep everything" and the writing clerk says "write nothing", the entry made fifty steps ago is
still there, untouched, and the route by which blame travels back to it passes through nothing but
fifty decisions to keep. That route is the carousel.

There is a catch, and this chapter spends some time on it. The clerks are trained too, and at the
start of training they act at random. A keeping clerk who keeps three-quarters of the ledger at each
step — which is what the customary initialization produces — has forgotten all but a millionth of
an entry within fifty steps, and the carousel does nothing. A writing clerk who writes half of every
passing note into the ledger fills it with noise long before anyone has learned what is worth
keeping. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber knew both; the second has a name in their paper, *internal state
drift*.

## The math: gates, a cell, and two gradients

Notation as in the notation guide: row vectors times weight matrices, rows = samples, superscript
$\langle t\rangle$ for time.

### The cell

All four of the cell's inputs come from one product of the step's input and the previous hidden
state, laid side by side in four blocks:

$$
z^{\langle t\rangle} = x^{\langle t\rangle} W_x + h^{\langle t-1\rangle} W_h + b, \qquad
i = \sigma(z_i),\;\; f = \sigma(z_f),\;\; g = \tanh(z_g),\;\; o = \sigma(z_o).
$$

The **input gate** $i$, the **forget gate** $f$ and the **output gate** $o$ are sigmoids, numbers
between 0 and 1; the **candidate** $g$ is what would be written. The cell keeps some of itself,
writes some of the candidate, and shows some of itself:

$$
c^{\langle t\rangle} = f \odot c^{\langle t-1\rangle} + i \odot g, \qquad
h^{\langle t\rangle} = o \odot \tanh\big(c^{\langle t\rangle}\big).
$$

With $i = 0$ and $f = 1$, $c^{\langle t\rangle} = c^{\langle t-1\rangle}$ exactly, whatever arrives. (The 1997 cell had
$f = 1$ always.) Four blocks of weights instead of one: an LSTM layer has four times the parameters
of a recurrent layer of the same width.

### Backpropagation through the cell

Walking backwards, a step now receives two gradients from the future — through the hidden state,
which the next step's gates read, and through the cell, which the next step keeps:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{\langle t\rangle}} = dH^{\langle t\rangle} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{\langle t+1\rangle}} W_h^{\top},
\qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial c^{\langle t\rangle}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial c^{\langle t+1\rangle}} \odot f^{\langle t+1\rangle}
+ \frac{\partial L}{\partial h^{\langle t\rangle}} \odot o \odot \big(1 - \tanh^2 c^{\langle t\rangle}\big).
$$

The product rule then gives each block its share, $dz_i = dc\,g\,i(1-i)$, $dz_f = dc\,c^{\langle t-1\rangle} f(1-f)$,
$dz_g = dc\,i\,(1-g^2)$, $dz_o = dh\,\tanh(c)\,o(1-o)$, and the shared weights sum their shares over
every step, exactly as in Module 06.

The second equation is the chapter. Along the cell, one step back multiplies the gradient by $f$ —
not by a weight matrix, not by an activation's slope, but by a number between 0 and 1 that the
network sets for itself, per unit and per step. Follow it back $k$ steps:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial c^{\langle t-k\rangle}} \supset \frac{\partial L}{\partial c^{\langle t\rangle}} \odot \prod_{j=0}^{k-1} f^{\langle t-j\rangle}.
$$

Module 06's gradient was a power of a matrix, and only a spectral radius of exactly one could keep a
power from vanishing or exploding. This one is a product of gates. If they are near one, it travels;
if they are smaller, it fades; and — since every factor is at most one — along the cell it can
*never* explode.

### The 1997 gradient

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber did not use the full gradient. Their algorithm truncated it wherever error
"wants" to leave a memory cell or a gate: the $W_h^{\top}$ term above was dropped at every step, and
error travelled back in time only along the carousel. It was cheap — it needs no stored history of
the sequence — and, the paper argued, it lost little, because outside the carousels the error decays
quickly anyway. In nanograd this is `backward(dH, cell_only=True)`; it is exactly the gradient of a
network whose gates read a *frozen copy* of $h^{\langle t-1\rangle}$, which is how the test suite checks it.

### The gate biases

At the start of training every gate is roughly the sigmoid of its bias. So the forget gate's bias
$b_f$ decides how much of the cell — and of the gradient along it — survives each step before the
network has learned anything, and the cell's memory initially lasts about

$$
\frac{1}{1 - \sigma(b_f)} = 1 + e^{b_f} \text{ steps:} \qquad 2 \text{ at } b_f = 0,\quad 3.7 \text{ at } b_f = 1,\quad 149 \text{ at } b_f = 5.
$$

A bias of 1 is the customary choice — the one Jozefowicz and colleagues found closes the LSTM's gap,
and the one Keras uses by default. The input gate's bias $b_i$ decides how much of every
passing input the cell collects while it waits. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's remedy for internal
state drift was "initially to bias the input gate toward zero."

## The code: one more gradient from the future

The C library gains one file
([`lib/c/nanograd/lstm.c`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/lib/c/nanograd/lstm.c)).
The heart of its backward pass carries both gradients from step $t+1$, and the `cell_only` flag cuts
one of them:

```c
for (int i = 0; i < m; i++)
    for (int j = 0; j < n_h; j++) {
        double dh = dh_out[i * n_h + j];                         /* from the read-out   */
        if (flows && !cell_only)
            for (int o = 0; o < n4; o++)
                dh += dz_next[i * n4 + o] * Wh[j * n4 + o];      /* via the next gates  */
        const double tc = tanh(c[i * n_h + j]);
        double d = dh * go * (1.0 - tc * tc);                    /* via h<t>            */
        if (flows)
            d += dc_next[i * n_h + j] * g_next[i * n4 + n_h + j];  /* along the cell    */
        dc[i * n_h + j] = d;
        dz[i * n4 + j]           = d * gg * (gi * (1.0 - gi));   /* input gate  */
        dz[i * n4 + n_h + j]     = d * cpv * (gf * (1.0 - gf));  /* forget gate */
        dz[i * n4 + 2 * n_h + j] = d * gi * (1.0 - gg * gg);     /* candidate   */
        dz[i * n4 + 3 * n_h + j] = dh * tc * (go * (1.0 - go));  /* output gate */
    }
```

The Python layer, `nanograd.LSTM`, is the same loop over time with the gates vectorized:

```python
for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
    dh = dH[t] + dh_next                          # from the output at t, and from t+1
    dc = dc_next + dh * o * (1.0 - TC[t] ** 2)    # along the carousel, and via h<t>
    dZ[t] = [dc * g * i * (1 - i), dc * c_prev * f * (1 - f),
             dc * i * (1 - g * g), dh * TC[t] * o * (1 - o)]   # four blocks
    dc_next = dc * f                              # one step back along the cell
    dh_next = 0 if cell_only else dZ[t] @ Wh.T    # ...and along h, unless cut
```

The C program
([`c/lstm.c`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/07-lstm/c/lstm.c))
trains four networks on the toy below from the same seeds, and is the bit-exact gate for all of
this.

## The gradient check, and C↔Python agreement

[`tests/test_agreement.py`](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/07-lstm/tests/test_agreement.py)
holds the usual three gates.

**Gradients.** The LSTM layer's $\partial L/\partial X$, $\partial L/\partial h_0$, $\partial L/\partial c_0$ and its three
weight gradients are finite-difference checked with an initial state and cell (errors around
$10^{-9}$). Truncation is checked against what it means — the sum of the full gradients of the
chunks, each starting from the state *and cell* the previous chunk left — to $4 \times 10^{-16}$. The
1997 gradient is checked against what *it* means: finite differences of the network whose gates
read a frozen $h^{\langle t-1\rangle}$, to $10^{-8}$. A small latch network and a small character model are
checked end to end, every parameter — compared as whole vectors, because an LSTM's gradient entries
span six orders of magnitude (an early step's share is scaled by every forget gate after it) and
finite differences resolve an entry only to about $10^{-11}$.

**Agreement.** C and Python train four networks on the latch for 300 Adam updates with clipping —
Module 06's RNN, an LSTM, the same LSTM with the 1997 gradient, and the same weights with the
textbook biases — and print a ten-number fingerprint for each: test loss, accuracy on sequences of
the training length and four times longer, the last batch loss and gradient norm, how often the clip
fired, a parameter checksum and three sentinel weights. They agree **to every printed digit**, for
all four, and a fifth line pins the C layer (initial state and cell, input gradients, truncation,
the 1997 gradient) to NumPy's. As in Module 06, the Python mirror evaluates `exp` and `tanh` through
the C library (Python's `math`), and its sigmoid uses the C's own two-branch formula.

**Two Python paths.** The vectorized library and the C-order mirror compute the same initial loss
and gradients for every twin to about $10^{-12}$, and the same parameters after three Adam steps to
about $10^{-13}$.

## Toy → real: the latch, then Shakespeare

**The latch.** Module 06's echo asked a network to hold $d$ symbols at once, so its limit mixed two
things: how far back the gradient reaches, and how much sixteen units can hold. This toy removes the
second. A signal, one of `A B C D`, arrives at step 0; then $T - 1$ distractors from `w x y z`; at the
last step the network must say which signal it saw. Two bits, whatever $T$ is — but the only error
signal is at the end, and the moment of keeping is at the start. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi called
the ability *information latching*; in miniature, it is task 2c of the 1997 paper.

**Four twins.** At $T = 80$, sixteen units, a fresh batch of 32 sequences per update, Adam with
clipping, 300 updates, the same batches for all four:

| twin | network | gate biases (input, forget) | gradient | accuracy, $T = 80$ | at $T = 320$ | clipped |
|------|---------|----------------------------|----------|------:|------:|----:|
| `rnn` | Module 06's RNN | — | full | 0.25 | 0.25 | 4 |
| `forget1` | LSTM | 0, **1** (textbook) | full | 0.27 | 0.20 | 0 |
| `lstm` | LSTM, same weights | **−5, +5** | full | **1.00** | 0.77 | 45 |
| `carousel` | LSTM, same weights | −5, +5 | **cell only** | **1.00** | **1.00** | 2 |

The RNN and the textbook LSTM end at chance (25%). The LSTM whose cells start open to the past
($\sigma(5) = 0.993$) and shut to new input ($\sigma(-5) = 0.007$) learns the task — and so does its twin
whose error never travels back through $h$ at all: everything that taught step 0 to keep its symbol
rode the cell, 79 steps. The forward passes of the three LSTMs differ in one line of initialization
or not at all.

**How long a lag?** Across lags, three seeds each, up to 1,000 updates:

| lag $T$ | 10 | 20 | 40 | 80 | 160 | 320 |
|---------|---:|---:|---:|---:|----:|----:|
| RNN | 1.00 1.00 1.00 | 1.00 0.49 1.00 | 0.48 0.73 0.44 | 0.78 0.26 0.48 | 1.00 0.25 0.26 | 0.24 0.43 0.79 |
| LSTM, gates open | **1.00** ×3 | **1.00** ×3 | **1.00** ×3 | **1.00** ×3 | **1.00** ×3 | **1.00** ×3 |

The RNN holds the signal across ten steps every time and across twenty two times in three; from
forty on, one run in twelve gets there. In the rest it often gets partway — 44% to 79% — telling two
or three of the four signals apart. The notebook shows why with a picture: write four values into a
recurrent unit with $h = \tanh(2h + \ldots)$ and they fall onto its two attractors, A and B onto one, C
and D onto the other — a robust memory of one bit. Written into an LSTM cell with its input gate shut
and its forget gate open, all four stay where they were put for 200 steps. The LSTM solves all
eighteen runs, at every lag up to 320, in 100 to 360 updates.

**Why: the gradient through time.** Send a unit gradient into the last state of 80-step sequences
and measure how much reaches the *input* $k$ steps back — a measure that means the same thing for
both architectures — and, for the LSTMs, how much rides the cell. At initialization, relative to the
last step:

| | $k = 10$ | $k = 40$ | $k = 79$ (the signal) |
|-|---------:|---------:|---------:|
| RNN (Xavier) | 0.15 | $1.2 \times 10^{-3}$ | $3.4 \times 10^{-6}$ |
| LSTM, forget bias 1 — input | 0.19 | $6.4 \times 10^{-3}$ | $8.6 \times 10^{-5}$ |
| LSTM, forget bias 1 — along the cell | 0.21 | $8.3 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.1 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| LSTM, gates open — input | 0.19 | 0.15 | **0.11** |
| LSTM, gates open — along the cell | 0.93 | 0.75 | **0.59** |

With a forget bias of 1 the carousel leaks a quarter of the gradient at every step, and the textbook
LSTM is barely better than the RNN. With the gates open the product of forget gates is
$\sigma(5)^{79} = 0.59$, and a tenth of the last step's gradient reaches the signal. The forward pass,
remember, could carry the symbol in all three.

**Two biases, two jobs.** The forget bias sets the cell's initial memory, $1 + e^{b_f}$ steps; the
input bias keeps the cell from collecting noise while it waits. Measured at initialization with a
forget bias of 5 and the latch's distractors streaming in: with an input bias of 0, the mean $|c|$
grows from 0.15 after one step to 5.8 after 80 and 14.8 after 320 — the cell is mostly the sum of the
distractors, and $\tanh$ has saturated ($1 - \tanh^2 c = 0.11$); with an input bias of −5 it stays at 0.1,
and the slope at 0.98. On the latch, two seeds each, first one knob — $b_f = b$, $b_i = -b$, the pairing
Tallec and Ollivier's "chrono" initialization uses — then the input bias alone at $T = 320$:

| $b_f = b,\ b_i = -b$ | memory at init | $T = 20$ | $T = 80$ | $T = 320$ |
|----------------------|---------------:|------:|------:|------:|
| $b = 0$ | 2 steps | 0.21 0.27 | 0.30 0.27 | 0.24 0.24 |
| $b = 1$ | 3.7 | **1.00 1.00** | 0.29 0.27 | 0.28 0.24 |
| $b = 2$ | 8.4 | **1.00 1.00** | 0.25 0.25 | 0.27 0.24 |
| $b = 3$ | 21 | **1.00 1.00** | **1.00 1.00** | 0.24 0.24 |
| $b = 5$ | 149 | **1.00 1.00** | **1.00 1.00** | **1.00 1.00** |
| $b = 7$ | 1,098 | **1.00 1.00** | **1.00 1.00** | **1.00 1.00** |

| $b_f = 5$, $T = 320$ | $b_i = 0$ | $-1$ | $-2$ | $-3$ | $-5$ |
|----------------------|------:|-----:|-----:|-----:|-----:|
| accuracy (two seeds) | 0.77 0.24 | 0.24 0.24 | 0.44 0.24 | **1.00 1.00** | **1.00 1.00** |

Each row learns lags up to a few times its initial memory, and none beyond. And a forget gate held
open is not enough on its own at $T = 320$: the input gate must start nearly shut too. Both are the
1997 design. Its cell could not forget at all, and its paper prescribed biasing the input gates
toward zero; the customary forget bias of 1 keeps neither.

**The carousel alone — and why it cannot explode.** Eight seeds, both gradients, 300 updates each
at $T = 80$:

| | solved | mean update | accuracy at $4T$ | clipped updates (per seed) | largest $\|g\|$ |
|-|-------:|------------:|------:|----------------|------:|
| full BPTT | 8 / 8 | 119 | 0.97 | 27, 12, 11, 16, 12, 13, 11, 11 | 46.3 |
| cell only (1997) | 8 / 8 | 119 | **1.00** | 7, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 0, 0 | **1.5** |

The two learn equally fast. But the full gradient spikes past the clipping threshold a dozen times
or more in every run — up to 46 times the threshold — while the cell-only gradient almost never
needs clipping. The carousel's equation says why: along the cell the gradient is multiplied by
forget gates between 0 and 1, so it can fade but it cannot grow. Every spike comes through the $h$
path, $dz^{\langle t+1\rangle} W_h^{\top}$ — a recurrent network's product of matrices, with all of Module 06's
cliffs. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's truncation removed exactly the part of the gradient that can
blow up and kept the part that carries the memory. (Graves and Schmidhuber returned to the full
gradient in 2005, and every framework computes it today — with clipping.)

**Inside the trained latch.** Not the textbook picture. The input gate never opens at the signal —
it is *lower* there (0.028) than at the distractors (0.081) — so step 0 leaves only a faint mark in the
cell, a few hundredths. The forget gate stays near 1 (0.984 on average), so the mark survives; and
after twenty-odd steps each step writes a little more in the direction of what is already stored. In
the cell that best separates the signals, A and B fan out in opposite directions while C and D stay
near zero (other cells split those two), and the mark grows sixtyfold by step 79, to where the
read-out can use it. A memory that is kept, then amplified; gradient descent finds whatever works.
The same amplifier runs backwards: in the trained network the gradient reaching the signal is
*larger* than the last step's.

**Tiny Shakespeare.** Module 06's character model, with the RNN swapped for an LSTM sized to the same
parameter budget — 122 cells give 99,739 parameters against the RNN's 99,137 — and the customary
forget bias of 1 (text gives an error signal at every step; nothing has to be carried blind). Module
06 trained its RNN at a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-3}$, and at that rate the LSTM *loses*. An
architecture compared at another one's learning rate is not compared fairly, so both were trained at
five:

| validation loss (nats/char) | $2 \times 10^{-3}$ | $4 \times 10^{-3}$ | $8 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.6 \times 10^{-2}$ | $3.2 \times 10^{-2}$ |
|------------------|------:|------:|------:|------:|------:|
| RNN, 256 units | 1.654 | **1.644** | 1.769 | 2.291 | 3.370 |
| LSTM, 122 cells | 1.724 | 1.656 | 1.624 | **1.620** | 1.667 |

Each at its best, the LSTM wins: **1.620 nats per character** (2.34 bits) against 1.644 — 0.024 at
the same budget. The two want very different steps: the RNN stalls at $1.6 \times 10^{-2}$ and learns
nothing at $3.2 \times 10^{-2}$, where the LSTM is within 0.05 of its best. (An LSTM as wide as the RNN —
256 cells, 346,433 parameters — reaches 1.582 even at Module 06's rate.) Both fit their training text
far better than the validation text; the LSTM's training loss ends near 1.36.

twentieth:

By ear, much like Module 06's: 0.024 nats per character is not a difference you hear. Two
measurements show where it lives.

**How far back does each model look?** Start each from the zero state $L$ characters before a
position of the validation text, let it read them, and score its prediction of the next character,
averaged over 4,096 positions:

| context $L$ | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 | 512 |
|-------------|--:|--:|---:|---:|---:|----:|----:|
| RNN | 2.235 | 1.720 | **1.631** | 1.631 | 1.631 | 1.631 | 1.631 |
| LSTM | 2.697 | 1.825 | 1.656 | 1.618 | **1.603** | 1.604 | 1.604 |

The RNN's prediction stops improving after 16 characters; nothing it read further back changes its
guess. The LSTM's keeps improving out to 64 — and stops there, at the length of the window it was
trained through: the gradient that taught it never reached further. Those 48 characters are its
advantage. (Started cold, the LSTM is worse with sixteen characters of context or fewer; its cells normally
hold values far from zero, and the zero state is one it rarely saw in training.)

**A cell that counts.** Karpathy, Johnson and Fei-Fei found character-level LSTM cells with jobs —
one turned on inside quotation marks, one tracked the position in the line. Tiny Shakespeare's lines
are verse, a few dozen characters each. For every validation character take the number of
characters since the last newline, and ask how well the network's state tracks it. One cell,
number 11, correlates at +0.79 on its own, and a linear read-out of all 122 cells recovers the
position in the line with $R^2 = 0.81$; from the RNN's 256 units, $R^2 = 0.36$, and its best single unit
manages 0.35. Over a passage the cell is a sawtooth: every newline knocks it down to about −3, and it
climbs back toward zero, character by character, until the next one. Its forget gate stays near 1
inside a line (0.93 on average) and dips at newlines (0.75) — deepest at the speaker names, where one
speech ends and another begins. Counting is what an additive cell does naturally: hold $f$ near 1 and
add a little at every step. A unit squashed through $\tanh$ at every step does it badly.

The [notebook](https://github.com/shaia/deep-learning-from-scratch/blob/main/topics/07-lstm/notebook.ipynb)
closes with the frameworks. PyTorch checks strictly: `torch.nn.LSTM` given our weights — its gate
blocks are in our order, $i, f, g, o$, and it keeps two biases that are always added together — returns
our states, our last cell and all of our gradients, including $\partial L/\partial c_0$, to $3 \times 10^{-14}$;
our truncation equals `.detach()` on $(h, c)$ every $k$ steps; and the 1997 gradient is one more
`.detach()`, on the $h^{\langle t-1\rangle}$ the gates read, to $2 \times 10^{-14}$. TensorFlow's `LSTM` on the
latch at $T = 80$ makes the chapter's point in one line each: with Keras's default biases (it adds 1
to the forget gate's) it ends at 25%; with a `Constant` bias initializer of −5 for the input gate and
+5 for the forget gate, at 100%. And Keras builds a character LSTM at the same budget — an
`Embedding`, an `LSTM(128, return_sequences=True)`, a `Dense` — that reaches 1.732 nats in four epochs
of stateless 64-character windows.

## What it couldn't do

The LSTM did not repeal Module 06's lesson; it changed what limits it. A network still learns only
what its training gradient reaches — truncate it at 64 characters and the LSTM's use of context
stops at 64 — but along the cell the gradient no longer has to fight a power of a matrix, so it
reaches as far as the forget gates let it. That condition is the chapter's other lesson. The
carousel works only if the cell starts out remembering, and the customary initialization does not
start it there: the 1997 cell, which could not forget, and its paper's input gates biased shut were
not incidental.

The cost did not go away either. An LSTM step is four recurrent steps' worth of arithmetic, and it
is still a step: the loop over time runs in order, each step waiting for the last. On text at this
scale the gain was real but modest — most of what predicts the next character is in the last few —
and both models fit their training text far better than text they had not seen.

Simpler gated cells followed. Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues' *gated recurrent unit* (2014) merges the
cell into the state and the input and forget gates into one "update" gate; Greff's ablations and
Jozefowicz's search found it about as good. Both keep the essential thing: a state that is
interpolated, not rewritten.

## Exercises & what's next

1. **A gated recurrent unit.** Implement Cho et al.'s GRU — $h^{\langle t\rangle} = (1-u) \odot h^{\langle t-1\rangle} + u \odot \tilde h$
   with an update gate $u$ and a reset gate inside $\tilde h$ — forward and backward, gradient-checked,
   and run the latch sweep. Which bias plays the role of the forget bias, and what value does the
   latch need?
2. **The 1997 cell.** Remove the forget gate ($f \equiv 1$) and train the latch with input bias −5. Then
   feed it one continuous stream with signals at random times and a question after each — no resets
   between episodes. Plot $|c|$ over a few thousand steps. What did Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins add,
   and why?
3. **Chrono initialization.** Draw each cell's forget bias as $\log u$, $u$ uniform on $[1, T_{\max} - 1]$,
   with $b_i = -b_f$ (Tallec and Ollivier 2018), and rerun the bias table with $T_{\max} = 320$. Does a
   spread of memory lengths beat a single bias at short lags, long lags, or both?
4. **Does the carousel amplify?** Train the latch with `cell_only=True` and repeat the "inside the
   trained latch" analysis. Does the cell-only network amplify its mark through $h$ the same way —
   and if it does, how did it learn to, when no gradient flows along $h$?
5. **A longer window.** Train the character LSTM with a 256-character window (16 streams instead of
   64, so each update still sees 4,096 characters) and redo the context curve. Does the plateau move
   from 64 to 256? Does the RNN's, from 16?
6. **Find another cell.** Search the trained character model's cells for one that tracks something
   else — being inside a speaker's name, the parity of a line, the distance since the last period.
   Correlation finds a cell; what experiment would show the model *uses* it?

A recurrent network's memory is a state it must rebuild at every step; an LSTM's is a cell it is
allowed to leave alone. The gradient travels along whatever is left alone.
